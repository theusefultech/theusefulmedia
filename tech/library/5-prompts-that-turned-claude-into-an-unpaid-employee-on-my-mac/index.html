5 prompts that turned Claude into an unpaid employee on my Mac

Steal these, then meet the vault they came from

Most people using Claude are stuck for one reason, and it isn't the one they think. It isn't that they can't write a good prompt. It's that they don't know what's possible to ask for. They treat a tool that can reorganize a thousand files and build a working dashboard like a slightly clever search box.

So today, no theory. Here are five prompts I actually use, each one a finished instruction you can paste into Claude on your Mac right now. Steal all five. They'll do more to change how you work than anything I could write about them.

1. The one that cleans the folder you've been avoiding

Everyone has the folder. Mine was Downloads, and it had grown to 230 items of pure chaos. This prompt sorts it while you watch, and crucially, it never deletes anything.

The magic word is plan. You see exactly what it intends to do before a single file moves, and you can change your mind in plain English. I ran this on my real Downloads folder and it sorted all 230 items into clean, labeled folders in about nine minutes.

2. The one that turns a pile of receipts into a spreadsheet

This is the prompt that made me a believer. A folder of receipts, some PDFs, some photos of paper, becomes a categorized expense report in two minutes.

That last sentence is the whole trick. Telling Claude to flag uncertainty instead of guessing is the difference between a spreadsheet you trust and one you have to audit. Keep that clause in every prompt that processes a pile of anything.

3. The one that reads your data and tells you what to ask

You have more data than you think: bank exports, sales numbers, your fitness log. The reason you never open them is that questioning a wall of rows is intimidating. So don't start with questions. Start here.

That last line is my favorite trick in my entire library. Someone who just read all your rows knows what's worth asking better than you do. Half my best findings started as Claude's suggested questions, not mine. This exact prompt is how I discovered that 59% of my own revenue comes from repeat readers.

4. The one that turns one article into five

If you make anything, this one compounds. A finished piece contains four or five smaller pieces, and pulling them out used to be a 90-minute chore I resented.
